I started building websites for brands and agencies, and over the years that grew into enterprise AEM platforms for global companies. Campaign landing pages, data-capture promotions, multi-brand sites — a lot of deadlines, a lot of markets, a lot of stakeholders. Looking back, the technical skills were the easy part. The lessons that stuck were about how software actually gets shipped and maintained.

## Deadlines teach you what matters

Campaign work runs on immovable dates. A promotion launches when the TV spot airs, not when the code feels finished. That pressure is a brutal but effective teacher: it forces you to separate what is essential from what is merely nice. You learn to build the resilient, correct core first and to treat polish as a layer you add if time allows — not the other way around. It also teaches you to say no to complexity you cannot afford to maintain after launch.

## The person configuring the site is a real user

Early on I thought of "users" as the people visiting the page. Then I spent years watching content teams struggle with components that made perfect sense to me and none to them. The author configuring a page at the last minute is a user too, and often the one whose experience determines whether the project stays healthy. Designing for that person — clear dialogs, safe defaults, forgiving components — quietly prevents a huge share of production problems.

## Fundamentals outlast frameworks

I have shipped production work in WordPress, custom PHP, React, Next.js and AEM. The tools changed completely; the things that made the work good did not. Semantic HTML, accessible markup, understanding how the browser renders and loads, clean separation of concerns, readable code someone else can pick up — these transfer across every stack I have touched. Frameworks are worth learning well, but they are a means. The fundamentals are the thing that compounds over a career.

## Communication is an engineering skill

The projects that went badly were rarely defeated by a hard technical problem. They were defeated by unclear expectations, a decision no one wrote down, or a requirement that surfaced too late. Learning to ask the awkward clarifying question early, to document the decision that everyone will forget, and to explain a tradeoff to a non-technical stakeholder saved more projects than any refactor I ever did. On distributed teams across several countries and time zones, this stopped being optional.

## Craft is a long game

It is tempting, especially under deadline pressure, to leave the mess and move on. Sometimes you have to. But the code you write today is code you or a teammate will meet again — during the next campaign, the next brand rollout, the next incident at an inconvenient hour. Small, consistent investments in clarity pay back disproportionately over years. Nobody remembers the shortcut that saved an afternoon; everyone remembers the module that was a pleasure to extend.

Ten years in, the throughline is simple: build for the humans on both sides of the screen, respect the fundamentals, and treat maintainability as part of the job rather than a luxury. The frameworks will keep changing. That part won't.
